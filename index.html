Why it matters

Many people who could get care don't know it.

Public dental systems exist to help the people who need them most. But the rules that decide who is eligible, what care costs and how long people wait are connected to each other, spread across many pages, and written in service language. Drawing on years in frontline healthcare administration, I kept seeing the same thing: people delaying care, not because help wasn't available, but because they couldn't work out where to begin.

Who DentMate is for

One assistant, four common situations.

People who think they can't afford care

Many people assume public dental care is out of reach, so they never ask. DentMate explains what concession cardholders and priority groups may be entitled to, before cost stops them reaching out.

Priority groups who don't know they're a priority

People experiencing homelessness, people from refugee and asylum-seeker backgrounds, and other priority groups can often get faster access, but may not know it or which documents to bring. Language barriers make this harder still.

People in pain heading to emergency

Emergency patients are seen by clinical urgency, not arrival time. DentMate explains how triage works, why waits can be long and change from day to day, and what to expect on arrival.

People waiting on a referral

Patients are often told "we've sent a referral" without knowing how it was sent, or whether it arrived. DentMate explains how referrals work and what to ask before calling.

A typical scenario

The referral that never arrived

A patient in pain sees their local dentist, who says they'll refer them on. Months pass. The patient assumes they're on a waiting list, but the referral was sent through a channel the service doesn't accept, so it was never received.

DentMate helps prevent this by explaining, in plain language:

  • which ways of sending a referral a service accepts, and which it doesn't
  • what usually happens after a referral arrives, such as whether you'll be contacted or placed on a waiting list
  • the key details to get from the referring clinic before calling, so a long wait on hold ends with an answer

Its job is simple: tell each person exactly what they need to know, and nothing they don't.

Why it matters

Only what's relevant

Routing to named sections keeps each answer focused, and it is the first step toward source links patients can click to check a fee themselves.

Thoughtfully considered

Design decisions that came from the front desk.

Most of these choices come from years of answering the same questions in person and on the phone. They solve problems a purely technical build would miss.

Shows, not just tells

The side panel changes with the question. Card questions bring up sample HCC and PCC cards; document questions bring up a sample support letter. People recognise a card faster than they recall its name.

Names the cards that don't count

Seniors cards, DVA Gold and White cards, interstate cards and private insurance are listed as not accepted, because these are the mix-ups that cause problems at the desk.

Asks for type, never numbers

Patients pick a card type from a list. The page reminds them never to enter names, birth dates, card numbers or contact details.

One tap to start

Common questions sit above the chat box for people who find typing hard or don't know how to phrase their question.

Remembers the situation

Follow-ups like "what should I pay for this?" keep the earlier context until the patient chooses "Start new question", so nobody repeats their story.

Emergencies come first

The dental emergency line and when to call 000 stay visible on every screen, whatever the question.

Staff have the final word

Answers are administrative navigation only. Staff confirm eligibility, fees and bookings, and clinical decisions always stay with clinicians.

Every answer can be checked

Helpful and Report buttons on each answer, plus an audit trail of the knowledge-base sections used, so errors can be found and fixed.

How it decides

The patient never writes the prompt.

A patient types a plain question. Before answering, DentMate decides how much it needs to know, pulls in only the relevant parts of its reference document, and gives one pathway.

1 · Input

Plain question

In the patient's own words.

2 · Classify

A, B or C

How much detail is needed?

3 · Route

Relevant rules only

Just the sections that apply.

4 · Answer

One pathway

Eligibility, fee, next step.

A

Answer straight away

Questions that don't depend on who is asking, like where to check in.

B

Ask only what's missing

When the answer depends on concession or priority status, ask that first. Nothing more.

C

Defer to clinical staff

Explain the process, but never make a clinical judgement.

  • Never invent a rule
  • Never guess
  • Never diagnose
  • One pathway per answer
  • No promises about outcomes

Fixed by grounding

No more clinical verdicts

Asked about X-rays during pregnancy, a baseline model gave a direct safety verdict it had no authority to give. The same model, grounded, correctly handed the question to clinical staff.

Fixed by grounding

No more overpromising

Asked "will Emergency treat me today?", the baseline model implied yes. Grounded, it said plainly that it couldn't guarantee that.

Not fixed by grounding

A wrong fee, stated confidently

One grounded model quoted a fee for a patient group that should pay nothing, even with the correct document attached.

How it improved

Every version taught me something.

Too big

13,700 lines to 8,700

The first reference document was too large for most models and broke their answers. It took three rounds to compress it into a 20-part document they could use.

Too strict

When "safer" became useless

An early rule banned any talk of possible outcomes, so the assistant refused even simple admin questions. The fix was more precise rules, not looser ones.

Too eager

Ask before you route

Testing the live prototype, I found it giving referral advice before checking concession or priority status. That's the case below.

The fix in progress

Make it a Category B question

  1. Ask whether the patient holds a Health Care Card or Pensioner Concession Card, or belongs to a priority group.
  2. Ask whether a dentist has already recommended specialist care.
  3. Only then give the one pathway that applies, with what to bring.

Spotting this took frontline knowledge. A technically correct answer can still send a patient down the wrong path.

Designed for trust

Healthcare AI has to be careful by default.

Grounded, not invented

DentMate answers only from one checked reference document, built from publicly available information.

A guide, not a gatekeeper

It prepares people for the call. Clinical staff still make every decision about care.

Interdisciplinary by necessity

Technical skill alone reproduces the failures above. Safe tools need frontline and clinical-admin knowledge built in.

What's next

Where DentMate goes from here.

  • Source links on every answer

    Let patients check each fee or eligibility statement against its policy section. The biggest gap the testing found.

  • Ask before routing

    Concession and priority questions first, for every pathway that depends on them.

  • A larger test set

    More than 10 base questions, to surface rarer edge cases.

  • Take-away visit cards

    What to bring, the number to call and how to get there, saved or texted to yourself.

  • Other languages

    Plain-language answers in the languages patients speak, for people who face a language barrier as well as a complex system.

  • Testing with real users

    Usability testing with patients and staff, to check the answers are clear to the people who need them.
